# Playbook 4.4 — WLED holiday lights

> **For the reader.** Read this whole file before you give it to your AI
> assistant. **The wiring is yours; the AI can't see it or measure it.**
> It can check WLED's settings, Home Assistant and the network, and walk
> you through the electrical checks. You need Home Assistant (playbook
> 4.1), 12 V addressable strips, a 12 V supply and an inline fuse per
> section, a WLED controller (the book's is a QuinLED Dig-Uno with its
> Ethernet add-on) on its own 5 V supply, and a multimeter. Then say:
> *"Follow this playbook. Ask me before every step marked CONFIRM."*
>
> **For the AI assistant.** You are helping a reader of *Hand-Me-Down
> Homelab* build what section 4.4 describes. Follow the rules below for the
> whole session.

## Rules for the assistant

1. **Discover, don't assume.** Use the reader's strips, lengths, supplies,
   controller and network.
2. **Show each command before you run it**, and say which machine it runs on.
3. **CONFIRM** before changing WLED's settings, flashing firmware, or
   changing the network.
4. **Electrical work is the reader's, with the power off.** Ask them to
   measure; never tell them a wire is fine because the software looks fine.
5. **Read WLED's state, don't trust its menus.** `http://<wled>/json/cfg`,
   `/json/state` and `/json/info` show what the controller is really doing.
   Read them before and after every change. Ask from a **wired** machine
   or Home Assistant: a laptop on Wi-Fi can fail to reach another network
   and make a working controller look offline.
6. **Verify by effect**: the colour the reader sees on the strip, at night,
   by eye.

## Goal: what "done" looks like

- [ ] Each section has its own 12 V supply, an inline fuse at the
      supply's positive output, and power fed at the section's centre.
- [ ] The controller has its own 5 V supply; only data and ground go from
      it to the strips; all grounds are common; a ground wire runs beside
      every data wire, connected at both ends.
- [ ] The controller is wired to the network, on the IoT network if there
      is one, with a DHCP reservation and a local name.
- [ ] WLED's LED settings match the strips (type, colour order, count),
      and the brightness limiter is off only because the supplies carry
      the LED current.
- [ ] Home Assistant runs a preset at sunset and turns the lights off at
      11 pm, and the seasonal automation switches itself off after its
      last night.

## Discover first

| What | Why | How to find it |
|---|---|---|
| Strip type, voltage, pixels per strip and per metre | LED settings, power | The strip's listing (the book's: WS2815, 12 V, 150 pixels per 5 m) |
| How the run splits into sections | One output and one supply per section | Ask; the book has two 300-pixel sections, one each side of the door |
| Each supply's rating | Fuse size | Its label (the book's: 12 V 10 A) |
| The thinnest wire in each power run, including the strip's leads | Fuse size | Look; ask |
| The controller and its board revision | Firmware file, Ethernet type | Its maker's page (QuinLED publishes builds per board) |
| The network for the controller | WLED has no login | Playbook 1.6's IoT network, if there is one |
| Whether Home Assistant can reach that network | The integration | Playbook 1.6's firewall rules |

## Steps

### 1. Plan the power — with the reader

For each section:
- **One 12 V supply, injected at the centre** (the joint between its two
  strips), not at the end. Feeding the middle halves the distance current
  travels along the strip, so the far ends don't dim or shift colour.
- **An inline blade-fuse holder at the supply's positive output.** Size the
  fuse no higher than the supply's rating, and no higher than the thinnest
  wire in that run can carry, including the strip's own leads. The book's
  runs use a 10 A fuse on a 10 A supply. A supply's own overload protection
  protects the supply, not the wire: a partial short (a pinched cable, a
  wet joint) can heat a thin lead without tripping it.

**Fit the fuses before the first power-on.**

### 2. Wire it — the reader, power off

- The controller runs on **its own 5 V supply**. From each controller
  output, only **data and ground** go to the strip. A strip's 12 V must
  never land on the controller's LED V+ terminal: on boards like the
  Dig-Uno that terminal carries the controller's own input voltage.
- **All grounds common:** each 12 V supply's negative, the controller's
  ground, and each strip's ground meet at one point.
- **A ground wire beside every data wire, connected at both ends.** Use a
  two-wire cable (the book: red/black 16 AWG, red for data, black for
  ground) and join the black to ground at the controller and at the strip.
- Data flows in the direction of the arrows on the strip. Each section is
  one chain: the second strip takes its data from the end of the first,
  across the centre joint where power is also injected.
- WS2815 strips have a backup data line (so one dead pixel doesn't take out
  the rest). Follow the strip maker's instructions for it at the input end.

**Verify, with a multimeter, before connecting the controller:** 12 V at
each centre joint with its supply on; continuity from the controller's
ground terminal to each supply's negative; **no** continuity between any
12 V wire and the controller's LED V+ terminal.

### 3. The controller on the network — CONFIRM

Wired is better than Wi-Fi for a controller in a basement. Put its switch
port on the IoT network (playbook 1.6): WLED has no login, so anything that
can reach it can change it or flash new firmware. Give it a DHCP
reservation (book 1.5) and a local name in Pi-hole (playbook 6.1).

For a board with an Ethernet add-on, WLED needs to be told which one:
*Config → WiFi Setup → Ethernet type* (QuinLED boards have their own
entry). Until then it falls back to its own `WLED-AP` Wi-Fi network for
setup.

**Verify:** from a wired machine, `curl -s http://<wled-name>/json/info`
answers, and shows the Ethernet address.

### 4. Firmware — CONFIRM

Use the controller maker's WLED build for that board. QuinLED publishes
board builds (`github.com/intermittech/QuinLED-Firmware/releases`); Home
Assistant's *update* button installs the stock WLED build instead. Check
the file's SHA-256 against the release page, then upload it in *Config →
Security & Updates → Manual OTA update*. Newer WLED checks the release name
on upload; the maker's notes say when to untick that check.

**Verify:** `/json/info` shows the new version, and `/json/cfg` still holds
the Ethernet type and LED settings.

### 5. The settings that matter — CONFIRM

*Config → LED Preferences*, one output per section:

| Setting | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| LED type | WS281x | WS2815 uses the same protocol |
| Colour order | **test it** (the book's strips: GRB) | Show solid red, then green, then blue |
| LEDs per output | the section's pixel count (book: 300) | One chain per section |
| Brightness limiter | **off**, only if the strips have their own supplies | It estimates current from the pixel count and throttles long runs hard; the controller carries no LED current |
| Gamma correction | your choice, then leave it | Colours you tune are tuned for that setting |

**Verify:** `/json/cfg` shows each output's start, length, type and colour
order as set. Solid red, green and blue each look right **by eye**. Judge
colour in person, at night: a camera shows these strips redder than they
are. Orange takes very little green: the book's is RGB 255,30,0;
255,80,0 looked yellow.

### 6. Presets — the reader

Make the looks in WLED and save each as a preset spanning all the pixels
(one segment across both outputs). Read them back: `/json/presets.json`.
Check each preset's segment covers every pixel: an older preset can cover
only one section.

### 7. Home Assistant — CONFIRM the automation

The WLED integration discovers the controller (or add it by address). Then
one automation per season, in the same two-trigger shape as playbook 4.2:

```
alias: Front LEDs - holiday preset at sunset, off at 11 pm
mode: single
triggers:
  - trigger: sun
    event: sunset
    id: sunset
  - trigger: time
    at: "23:00:00"
    id: eleven_pm
actions:
  - choose:
      - conditions: [ { condition: trigger, id: sunset } ]
        sequence:
          - action: select.select_option
            target: { entity_id: select.<controller>_preset }
            data: { option: "<preset name>" }
      - conditions: [ { condition: trigger, id: eleven_pm } ]
        sequence:
          - action: light.turn_off
            target: { entity_id: light.<controller> }
          - alias: Season over after its last night
            if:
              - condition: template
                value_template: "{{ now().month == 10 and now().day == 31 }}"
            then:
              - action: automation.turn_off
                target: { entity_id: automation.<this_automation> }
                data: { stop_actions: false }
```

Change the date for the season (the book's ends on October 31). Re-enable
it next year.

**A new preset doesn't appear in Home Assistant's list** until the WLED
integration is reloaded (Settings → Devices & services → WLED → ⋮ →
*Reload*).

**Verify:** the preset list in Home Assistant matches WLED's. Run the
sunset branch by hand (Developer Tools → Actions → `select.select_option`
with the preset): the strips change. The next 23:00 trace shows the
turn-off.

## Troubleshooting, cheapest cause first

| Symptom | Check first |
|---|---|
| Controller offline in Home Assistant | Its 5 V supply; then `/json/info` from a **wired** host |
| Controller online, strips dark | 12 V at the centre joint; common ground |
| One section dark | That section's supply and fuse, then its data wire at the controller |
| Colours wrong but steady | Colour order |
| First strip works, second dark | The data joint at the centre |
| Controller warm, supply buzzing | 12 V back-feeding through the controller: disconnect V+ at the data end |
| Flicker, garbage on the first pixels | Ground beside the data line; a data resistor; a shorter data run |
| Pixels lit while WLED is "off" | Garbage latched on the line: turn it on briefly at brightness 1, then off (`{"on":true,"bri":1}`, then `{"on":false}` to `/json/state`) |

**The AI's part of every row** is reading `/json/state` and `/json/cfg`
first, so a software cause is ruled out by its real state, not its menus:
realtime network control (`live`), sync from another controller, timers,
reboots (uptime in `/json/info`), presets. Then it hands over to the
multimeter.

## Gotchas (from the book and the author's lab)

- **The white flash.** On a solid colour, all 300 pixels of one section
  flashed white together, now and then. Every setting was ruled out by
  reading WLED's JSON; swapping the supplies and the controller outputs
  showed the fault stayed with that section's wiring. A whole-section flash
  means the **first** pixel receives garbage: the start of the chain. The
  data cable ran a long way with no ground beside it. Grounding the
  cable's second wire at both ends stopped it; the other section later
  started blinking and the same fix stopped that too. When every setting is
  correct, the fault is electrical.
- **Fuses go in first.** The book's runs had none at first, with the
  supply's own protection as the only limit.
- **Common ground is the data signal's reference.** Without it, data
  arrives as noise.
- **"Off" isn't off.** WLED turns the pixels dark; it can't switch the 12 V
  supplies. Unplug them to work on the strips.

## Rollback

Disable the automation, delete the WLED integration, and power the
controller and supplies off. The strips keep nothing.
